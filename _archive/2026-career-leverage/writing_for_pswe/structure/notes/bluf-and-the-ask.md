# BLUF & the ask

**Mental model:** a director's inbox is a router, not a reading chair. Each item gets a routing decision — approve / delegate / defer / ignore — within seconds. Your first sentence either hands them that decision or spends their 60 seconds making them reconstruct it. BLUF = Bottom Line Up Front: ask first, evidence after, in descending importance.

## Why it works
- Readers choose *how* to read in the first seconds (skim vs engage). Front-loaded structure survives skimming; build-up structure dies in it.
- Attention only decays — anything after sentence 3 gets a fraction of the reader you had at sentence 1.
- Writer-side forcing function: if you can't write the ask sentence, the problem isn't wording — you haven't decided what you're asking for. ("Crucial to our throughput" is what comes out when the ask isn't crystallized.)

## The ask sentence carries three loads
1. The decision requested (approve X, fund Y, choose A over B)
2. The cost (money, headcount, time — a number)
3. The payoff (a number, or the mechanism that produces one)

Example (different domain): "Approve two weeks of pairing time to move order exports to the read replica: exports spike primary CPU 30% at peak, and the replica absorbs that at no extra infra cost."

## Practical consequences
- Inverted pyramid: the doc survives truncation at any paragraph — the reader who stops early still leaves with the decision.
- "Too long" complaints are usually structure complaints: readers tolerate length when the point came first; the same length infuriates when the point comes last.
- Closings don't carry asks. "Thanks" ends a pitch that never asked.

## Pitfall
BLUF ≠ zero context. One clause of context inside the ask sentence is fine ("To keep review latency flat as PR volume grows, I'm asking for…"). Three sentences of warm-up before the ask is not BLUF.
